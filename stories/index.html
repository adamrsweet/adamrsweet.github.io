Citrus Peaks in Winter

A pocket of summer in the gray of the year

There’s a moment every winter when the fruit bowl changes. The apples step aside, and suddenly there are clementines everywhere: in stockings, in lunch boxes, in a little pile of peels on the kitchen table.

It always strikes me as a small miracle. Just when the days are shortest here in Granby, oranges and tangerines from Florida groves and California hills hit their sweetest. Winter is citrus season.

This song is a love letter to that. The easy-peel joy of a clementine, kids with sticky hands in the schoolyard, juice by the fire. And a reminder that sometimes the best way to love something is one perfect section at a time.

The chorus says it for me:

“Citrus peaks in winter, sweet and bright,

Peel back the color, chase out the night.”

Next time you peel one, save a section for later.

The Westward Speech

How the Celtic languages may have traveled to the British Isles, and the song it gave me

This week, two studies in Nature tried to answer a question I’ve carried since graduate school: where did the Celtic languages come from, and how did they reach the British Isles?

An international team led by the University of Copenhagen and Leiden University read DNA from 4,587 ancient Europeans, spanning about 5,000 years. For Celtic, the best fit was a westward movement of people tied to the Urnfield culture of eastern central Europe, about 3,200 to 2,800 years ago. It reached France, the British Isles, northeastern Spain and Italy, right in the window when scholars think Proto-Celtic took shape.

The part that stopped me: in Britain, that migration left only a small genetic footprint. Most Iron Age ancestry there still traced to older groups. So the language may have spread less by replacing people than by neighbors borrowing from neighbors, newcomers and locals who could already half understand each other.

The researchers are careful about it. As senior author Eske Willerslev put it, “DNA sequences do not tell us directly which language people spoke.” These are best-fit explanations, not proof.

That idea, speech settling in by both migration and borrowing, became The Westward Speech. From the chorus:

“Westward the speech and the people came,

And the old words settled like rain.”

Verica's Coin

The "king" who gave Rome its excuse to invade Britain may have been a queen

For a long time, the history books called Verica a king: ruler of the Atrebates in southern Britain, driven out around AD 40, who crossed to Rome and asked Emperor Claudius for help. The Roman historian Cassius Dio wrote that the invasion of Britain in AD 43 happened because Verica persuaded Claudius to cross the Channel.

This week, Dr. Miles Russell of Bournemouth University made the case that Verica was a woman.

The clues were hiding in plain sight. The coins carry the name VIR and the title REX, and for generations scholars read REX as proof of a man. Russell argues Rome used the title for client rulers of either sex, Cleopatra being the famous example. And while other British rulers of the time put obviously male warriors on their coins, Verica’s show a slender, bare-breasted rider with a spear. The name itself ends in “a,” like Boudica and Cartimandua.

Not everyone is convinced. Oxford’s Roger Tomlin told the BBC the coins alone don’t prove it. But I couldn’t stop thinking about that rider on the coin, looking the same for two thousand years while everyone decided what she was.

The chorus says it for me:

“Call her king if the story needs a man,

But the exile was a woman when she ran.”

Russell’s full argument is in his new book, Caratacus: Great King of Britain, out from Bloomsbury in January.

The Empty Quads of Hampshire

A song for the college that asked only for questions

Hampshire College opened in Amherst in 1970 with an idea most schools wouldn’t touch: no grades, just questions you chose and followed as far as they went. I was one of the students who walked those paths. My first advisor was a biology professor. By my second semester I’d moved to Prescott, become a music major and found Ray Copeland, who was amazing. My first band, Pieces of Neck and the Broken Axes, started there.

This spring the trustees voted to close the college for good after the fall 2026 semester. Years of thin enrollment and debt that couldn’t be refinanced caught up with it. A loan this summer let the last students finish, about 170 of them, mostly seniors. In December the last classes end.

Now the talk is about land. The town of Amherst is studying how to rezone the campus’s 632 acres in town, with a plan expected early next year, and the college plans to sell parcels to pay its debts. This week a group of current and former students called Hampshire Commoning asked the town for a one-year pause. The Town Council sent their petition on to the Planning Board.

I wrote The Empty Quads of Hampshire in the week the news kept coming. It doesn’t offer hope, because I didn’t find any.

The wind is in the empty quads, the lights are going out.

They’ll pave the fields we wandered.

The future that we dreamed of will not come around again.

If you walked those paths too, what do you remember most?

Sheep, Bees, and Leeks: A Song for Upinngil Farm in Gill

The barnyard theory of inevitability, and a farm that keeps changing

Some farms start with a grand plan. Upinngil Farm in Gill started with a small one: sheep for the wool, bees for the honey, and leeks by the crate for the kitchen.

That was Clifford and Patricia Hatch’s idea back in the late ‘80s, on a hillside in Gill. It didn’t stay that simple. The leeks didn’t sell the way Clifford hoped, but people loved picking strawberries in the summer. So the farm followed the people. Then came cows and raw milk, then cheese, then a bakery and a farm store that’s open all year. The family has a name for it: the barnyard theory of inevitability.

What I love about this story is that nobody forced it. They planted what they knew, watched what their neighbors wanted, and kept changing. Their daughter Sorrel came home from college with ideas about farming more kindly, Isaac built the buildings, and the farm kept growing into something nobody planned at the start.

The chorus says it for me:

“Oh, sheep, bees, and leeks, that was the plan,

But the barnyard’s got a mind of its own, my friend.”

It’s a song about changing gears, and about a corner of the Pioneer Valley where people show up for each other.

If you’re up near Gill, stop by the farm store and say hello for me.

Harvest and Hearth

Seven Thanksgiving songs from the Pioneer Valley

Every November the valley does the same beautiful thing. The buses pull out of UMass, the campus goes quiet, the pumpkins stack up on the porches, and the Seven Sisters turn purple at sundown while the rest of us get the table ready. Harvest and Hearth is my record of that week: an album about the work of late autumn and the stillness that comes after it.

The songs

  • In Hadley Fields: the river running slow, pumpkins heavy in the fields, and the Holyoke Range keeping watch over the valley. The students are leaving, and the farmers keep working till the cold sets in.
  • We Give Thanks: a song for the old Strong House in Amherst, with its floors pulled up this fall so the sills and stone underneath can be shored up before the museum opens again in spring. Mabel and the Dickinson lines are in there too.
  • Pour The Cider: Baldwins and Macouns from the Franklin County hills, Cider Days coming. From Hawley to Hadley, from the ridge to the river.
  • Pumpkin Smash: hauling the jack-o'-lanterns out to Route 2 and dropping them thirty feet onto the ground at Hager's. We break what we grew just to start it again.
  • Thank You This Thanksgiving: for the firefighters in Chicopee who keep the station lights on through the holiday, and for the first woman to become deputy chief there.
  • Stuffing the Pantry: the Thanksgiving morning race in East Longmeadow, fifteen years of running so the Open Pantry shelves don't run bare.

My favorite line on the record comes from Stuffing the Pantry:

"We'll sit down to dinner with a fuller kind of grace

Happy Thanksgiving from Granby.

Moon Weaver

A song for Jared Isaacman, and the last track on Past the Sky

Yes, the title tips its hat to Gary Wright. "Dream Weaver" came out in 1975, and it has stayed with me for fifty years. When I started writing about Jared Isaacman, the kid who told his kindergarten teacher he was going to space, the word "weaver" wouldn't leave me alone.

The story

Jared left high school at 16, built a company in his parents' basement, learned to fly, became the first private citizen to walk in space, and now leads NASA as it gets ready to go back to the Moon. I wanted the song to move the way that life did: patient at first, then lifting.

My favorite lines are in the bridge:

"We left our footprints, then we left them behind
But the Moon never moved and the dream never died"

How it sits with my other space songs

Moon Weaver closes Past the Sky, my album of songs about NASA, the people who fly, and the machines we send up. Every song on it starts with a real story.

  • Come Lift Her Again: Hubble is slipping toward the atmosphere, and NASA is asking who can boost her back up.
  • Old 3I: the interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS, older than our Sun, carrying ice from before we had stars.
  • The Barrier's Been Breached: the May 2024 solar storm that tore a hole in the Van Allen belt.
  • Four Minutes: two bursts of light the Swift telescope caught four minutes apart, from the same patch of sky.
  • A Romantic Idea: did comets really bring the seeds of life to Earth? Probably not, and the song says so gently.
  • The Hidden One ED138: a small world past Pluto that the Rubin telescope found hiding in old pictures.
  • The Chartreuse Zombie Star: a dead star glowing green after swallowing its planets.

Moon Weaver is the odd one out. It's the only song about a person instead of a discovery, the longest by far at five and a half minutes. The others are news ballads. This one is a portrait.

If it carries you somewhere, pass it to someone who still looks up.

The Real Science Behind Past the Sky

Seven songs, seven discoveries, and the scientists who made them.

Every song on Past the Sky started with a real discovery. Here’s what’s behind each one.

Come Lift Her Again. After 36 years in orbit, the Hubble Space Telescope is slowly sinking toward Earth's atmosphere, and NASA is exploring how a commercial mission might boost her higher.

Old 3I. 3I/ATLAS is only the third object ever seen passing through our solar system from another star. A team led by Nathan Roth at NASA Goddard reported in Nature Astronomy on Oct. 23 that it carries methane, a clue to the chemistry of the place it came from.

The Barrier's Been Breached. During the great solar storm of May 2024, Japan's Arase satellite watched ultra-relativistic electrons push inside a region of the Van Allen belts long thought to be an impenetrable barrier. The study was led by Yoshizumi Miyoshi at Nagoya University.

Four Minutes. Two gamma-ray bursts came from the same patch of sky just minutes apart. The finding, published in Nature Astronomy in October, came from NASA's Swift observatory, which made its final observation on Oct. 20 after more than 20 years.

A Romantic Idea. Did comets bring life's building blocks to Earth? A Cambridge study led by Richard Anslow ran 100,000 simulated bombardment histories. In 54 percent of them, not a single comet delivered enough hydrogen cyanide. "It's kind of a romantic idea," Anslow said, "but our results suggest it's highly unlikely."

The Hidden One ED138. The Rubin Observatory's first dwarf planet candidate is a world beyond Neptune, somewhere between 600 and 1,500 kilometers across, found by a team including Matthew Holman of the Center for Astrophysics, Harvard & Smithsonian.

The Chartreuse Zombie Star. A dead star called WD J1330 glows an unusual color after swallowing material from its own planetary system. The work comes from Mark Hollands and colleagues at the University of Warwick.

The Hard Way

A song for two libraries, Housatonic’s Ramsdell and Amherst’s Jones

Two towns, one familiar melody of good intentions meeting hard municipal reality.

In the village of Housatonic, the 1908 Ramsdell Public Library is one of Great Barrington's few town-owned buildings on the National Register of Historic Places. Its renovation has stalled under pressures Amherst knows well from the Jones Library: state grant requirements pushing ambitious designs, historic preservation questions arriving late, no total cost estimate, and private fundraising hopes fading away.

Great Barrington also knows how to keep a community space. In the early 1920s, local Jewish farmers, cattlemen and shopkeepers founded Congregation Ahavath Sholom ("Love of Peace") and raised the money for their schoolhouse building with a klezmer concert. It is still the oldest continuously used synagogue building in Berkshire County.

Why I wrote the song

"The Hard Way" opens with a slow doina, a clarinet alone, like someone walking the empty stacks of a library whose future is still being argued. Then it moves into a freylekh that keeps circling the same phrase without quite resolving. It isn't a protest song, and it isn't a joke. It's the sound of towns that keep learning that history doesn't step aside just because the drawings are finished.

"Ramsdell, Ramsdell / Historic and waiting."

Tsunami Lament

An 8.8 earthquake, a warning across the Pacific, and the people who sound the alarm.

On July 29, 2025, I watched the news like a lot of people did: holding my breath for the whole Pacific.

What happened

A magnitude 8.8 earthquake struck off the east coast of Russia's Kamchatka Peninsula. Within about ten minutes, NOAA's Pacific Tsunami Warning Center in Honolulu had sent out its first tsunami threat message. Warnings and advisories followed for Hawaii, Alaska, the West Coast, Japan and countries all around the Pacific Rim.

In Hawaii, sirens sounded through the afternoon and people moved to higher ground. The waves came in the evening. The highest measured in Hawaii was about 1.7 meters (roughly 5.7 feet) at Kahului, Maui. It was serious, but nowhere near what people feared. The warning center kept watching through the night and canceled the last Hawaii advisory the next morning.

Why I wrote it

The next day I wrote "Tsunami Lament" for the people who fled in the night, the communities holding their breath, and the memory of the big ones that came before. It's also, quietly, a song for the people who stay up all night watching the tide gauges so the rest of us know when to run.

What they said

"It seriously made my day." — Charles McCreery, Director, Pacific Tsunami Warning Center

The bottom line

When a warning works, nothing much happens, and nobody writes a song about that. I wanted to.

Pickle Bot

A work song for the people who unload the trucks, and the small MIT crew building a robot to spare their backs

Almost everything you buy rides in the back of a truck. Somebody has to carry it out.

What happened

In 2018, three MIT alumni, AJ Meyer, Ariana Eisenstein and Dan Paluska, went to a UPS warehouse and watched 15 people unload trucks through a winter night shift. It's some of the hardest work there is. Boxes are heavy, trailers can be freezing or baking hot, and warehouse injury rates run more than twice the national average.

So they started the Pickle Robot Company, now based in Charlestown, Massachusetts. Their one-armed robots roll into a trailer and unload it on their own, picking up boxes of up to 50 pounds and setting them on a conveyor belt. The name is a nod to the Apple Computer Company. Early on, money was running low, and their first robot could only unload reliably for about 20 seconds at a time. They posted a video of it anyway, and the company grew from there.

MIT News told their story on December 5, 2025.

Why I wrote the song

"Pickle Bot" was born on a long, hot loading dock. It's a tribute to the folks who haul the heavy stuff, and to the small crew that dared to build a machine to spare some backs.

It's a song about work, about handing off the dull pain of drudgery, and about how one robot arm can point toward a better deal for everyone.

How you can help

And next time a package lands on your porch, think about the people who lifted it.

The bottom line

Good machines should take the worst jobs, so people can do better ones. Pickle is building toward that, right here in Massachusetts.

The observatory

In 2007, archaeologist Iván Ghezzi and archaeoastronomer Clive Ruggles showed in the journal Science that the Thirteen Towers of Chankillo were a solar calendar. Watched from the right spot, the sun rises and sets behind a different tower as the year turns. Built around 250 BC, it's the oldest known solar observatory in the Americas. The new discovery

In November 2025, Dr. Ghezzi, who directs the Chankillo project for Peru's Ministry of Culture, announced something new. Excavations that year found older structures at the site with an astronomical orientation, built with different techniques using stone, mud and adobe. The team also found a corridor aligned with the moon. Radiocarbon dating is still underway, so the exact age isn't known yet. But it means people may have been watching the sky here even before the towers, and watching the moon as well as the sun.

Stone Tools’ Trace

How a few chipped stones are rewriting the story of the First Americans

How did the first people get to North America? For a long time, the textbook answer was a land bridge and an ice-free corridor. The stone tools are telling a different story.

The research

In October 2025, a team including Oregon State University archaeologist Loren Davis published a study in the journal Science Advances. They compared stone tools from North American sites dated between about 20,000 and 13,500 years ago. They found that these tools share a style, and that the earliest projectile points of this kind show up around 20,000 years ago in Hokkaido, in northern Japan.

That supports the idea that the First Peoples of North America came from East Asia along the Pacific coast during the last ice age, likely using boats, before an inland route through the ice sheets had opened.

Davis put it this way: the study "puts the First Americans back into the global story of the Paleolithic – not as outliers – but as participants in a shared technological legacy."

I love a story where a small, patient clue changes the big picture. A flake of stone, shaped by someone's hands 20,000 years ago, can still tell us where they walked and sailed. "Stone Tools' Trace" follows that coast, "the sea to their right, ice to their side."

Under the Same Moon

On family, distance, and the places between places

My latest song, Under the Same Moon, is about families separated by circumstances beyond their control. It grew out of thinking about Korea, a country where the division between North and South has left generations of families unable to see one another, sometimes for the rest of their lives. Parents, children, brothers, and sisters may live only a relatively short distance apart, yet be separated by a border they cannot cross.

I’ve been thinking about separation quite a bit lately, although in a very different context.

I live on several acres in Granby, Massachusetts. There’s a garden, an old chicken coop, blueberry and raspberry bushes, jujube trees, an orchard, and a big maple that is currently shedding its leaves over a white plastic chair. Beyond the cultivated land is a swamp, full of brambles, mud, and places where walking isn’t particularly practical.

On the other side is Amherst Street, where the posted speed limit is 35 miles per hour but the traffic often moves considerably faster. Trucks pass close enough to create gusts of wind that can blow your hat off. There was a time when I walked down the shoulder to West Street, up Burnett, and down to the bridge every day. These days, with my mobility not what it used to be, I don’t feel safe doing that.

So I walk a loop around the property, sometimes two or three times a day. I know it so well that I can put water on the stove to boil, walk the circuit, and return just about when it’s ready.

It’s a beautiful place, but sometimes I feel walled in.

This is a strange sensation for someone who has spent much of his life exploring. As a child, I discovered forgotten stairwells, passages, catwalks, and abandoned sections of buildings. Later, after rebuilding an old Willys Jeep, I drove the wood roads around Nelson, Sullivan, and Marlborough, New Hampshire, without a map, just to see where they went.

I found places I loved, including Woodward Pond near Apple Hill. I brought musician friends there to swim. I remember one evening when a group of us visited an abandoned spring, arranging candles and flashlights along the roof beams so their light shone down through the slightly greenish water. Later we lay in a field among the hay and tall grass, looking at the stars.

I don’t remember what we talked about. I remember being there.

I’ve always been drawn to what I call edge places: the spaces most people pass without noticing, the roads that disappear into the woods, the questions nobody thinks to ask. I wasn’t looking for recognition or even necessarily looking for anything in particular. I wanted to know what was around the next corner.

Now my world is smaller, at least physically. But the curiosity hasn’t gone anywhere.

And neither has my sense of family.

I’ve been revisiting family photographs and memories recently: my father practicing cello, the old violin case he opened for me in our driveway when I was a small boy, my mother reading the newspaper aloud, my siblings, the instruments my father built, and the many different directions our lives have taken.

I’ve also been thinking about my own family—Emily, Richard, Bina, and the people connected to us through years of shared experience. Families are complicated. People grow up, move away, become absorbed in their own lives, and sometimes lose touch. Even when we love one another, we don’t always understand what another person is experiencing.

That kind of distance is nothing like the enforced separation of Korean families. I don’t want to pretend otherwise. There is an enormous difference between living far apart, feeling isolated, and being forbidden to cross a border to see someone you love.

But thinking about one kind of separation can make us more attentive to another.

We often imagine separation as a matter of miles. Yet someone can be across the world and remain close to us, while someone in the next room can feel impossibly far away. A road can be a barrier. So can illness, age, fear, politics, or simply the passage of time.

Music is one of the ways I’ve always tried to cross those distances.

I’ve played with people whose languages I didn’t speak, whose musical traditions were different from my own, and whose lives intersected with mine for only a few hours or days.
