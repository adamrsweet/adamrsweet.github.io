The Westward Speech

How the Celtic languages may have traveled to the British Isles, and the song it gave me

This week, two studies in Nature tried to answer a question I’ve carried since graduate school: where did the Celtic languages come from, and how did they reach the British Isles?

An international team led by the University of Copenhagen and Leiden University read DNA from 4,587 ancient Europeans, spanning about 5,000 years. For Celtic, the best fit was a westward movement of people tied to the Urnfield culture of eastern central Europe, about 3,200 to 2,800 years ago. It reached France, the British Isles, northeastern Spain and Italy, right in the window when scholars think Proto-Celtic took shape.

The part that stopped me: in Britain, that migration left only a small genetic footprint. Most Iron Age ancestry there still traced to older groups. So the language may have spread less by replacing people than by neighbors borrowing from neighbors, newcomers and locals who could already half understand each other.

The researchers are careful about it. As senior author Eske Willerslev put it, “DNA sequences do not tell us directly which language people spoke.” These are best-fit explanations, not proof.

That idea, speech settling in by both migration and borrowing, became The Westward Speech. From the chorus:

“Westward the speech and the people came,

And the old words settled like rain.”
