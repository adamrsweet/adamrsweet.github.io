Lost Observatory

Before Chankillo’s Thirteen Towers followed the sun, someone was watching the moon

On a ridge in Peru's Casma Valley stand thirteen stone towers in a row. For a long time nobody knew why.

The observatory

In 2007, archaeologist Iván Ghezzi and archaeoastronomer Clive Ruggles showed in the journal Science that the Thirteen Towers of Chankillo were a solar calendar. Watched from the right spot, the sun rises and sets behind a different tower as the year turns. Built around 250 BC, it's the oldest known solar observatory in the Americas. In 2021 UNESCO named Chankillo a World Heritage Site.

The new discovery

In November 2025, Dr. Ghezzi, who directs the Chankillo project for Peru's Ministry of Culture, announced something new. Excavations that year found older structures at the site with an astronomical orientation, built with different techniques using stone, mud and adobe. The team also found a corridor aligned with the moon. Radiocarbon dating is still underway, so the exact age isn't known yet. But it means people may have been watching the sky here even before the towers, and watching the moon as well as the sun.

Why I wrote it

I kept picturing moonlight threading through a narrow stone corridor long before the famous towers went up.

What they said

"We loved the song, thank you very much!"
— Dr. Iván Ghezzi, Director, Chankillo

The bottom line

People have always looked up and tried to keep time with the sky. At Chankillo they built it in stone, and the desert is still giving their work back to us.
