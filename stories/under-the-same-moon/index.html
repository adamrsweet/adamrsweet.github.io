Under the Same Moon

On family, distance, and the places between places

My latest song, Under the Same Moon, is about families separated by circumstances beyond their control. It grew out of thinking about Korea, a country where the division between North and South has left generations of families unable to see one another, sometimes for the rest of their lives. Parents, children, brothers, and sisters may live only a relatively short distance apart, yet be separated by a border they cannot cross.

I’ve been thinking about separation quite a bit lately, although in a very different context.

I live on several acres in Granby, Massachusetts. There’s a garden, an old chicken coop, blueberry and raspberry bushes, jujube trees, an orchard, and a big maple that is currently shedding its leaves over a white plastic chair. Beyond the cultivated land is a swamp, full of brambles, mud, and places where walking isn’t particularly practical.

On the other side is Amherst Street, where the posted speed limit is 35 miles per hour but the traffic often moves considerably faster. Trucks pass close enough to create gusts of wind that can blow your hat off. There was a time when I walked down the shoulder to West Street, up Burnett, and down to the bridge every day. These days, with my mobility not what it used to be, I don’t feel safe doing that.

So I walk a loop around the property, sometimes two or three times a day. I know it so well that I can put water on the stove to boil, walk the circuit, and return just about when it’s ready.

It’s a beautiful place, but sometimes I feel walled in.

This is a strange sensation for someone who has spent much of his life exploring. As a child, I discovered forgotten stairwells, passages, catwalks, and abandoned sections of buildings. Later, after rebuilding an old Willys Jeep, I drove the wood roads around Nelson, Sullivan, and Marlborough, New Hampshire, without a map, just to see where they went.

I found places I loved, including Woodward Pond near Apple Hill. I brought musician friends there to swim. I remember one evening when a group of us visited an abandoned spring, arranging candles and flashlights along the roof beams so their light shone down through the slightly greenish water. Later we lay in a field among the hay and tall grass, looking at the stars.

I don’t remember what we talked about. I remember being there.

I’ve always been drawn to what I call edge places: the spaces most people pass without noticing, the roads that disappear into the woods, the questions nobody thinks to ask. I wasn’t looking for recognition or even necessarily looking for anything in particular. I wanted to know what was around the next corner.

Now my world is smaller, at least physically. But the curiosity hasn’t gone anywhere.

And neither has my sense of family.

I’ve been revisiting family photographs and memories recently: my father practicing cello, the old violin case he opened for me in our driveway when I was a small boy, my mother reading the newspaper aloud, my siblings, the instruments my father built, and the many different directions our lives have taken.

I’ve also been thinking about my own family—Emily, Richard, Bina, and the people connected to us through years of shared experience. Families are complicated. People grow up, move away, become absorbed in their own lives, and sometimes lose touch. Even when we love one another, we don’t always understand what another person is experiencing.

That kind of distance is nothing like the enforced separation of Korean families. I don’t want to pretend otherwise. There is an enormous difference between living far apart, feeling isolated, and being forbidden to cross a border to see someone you love.

But thinking about one kind of separation can make us more attentive to another.

We often imagine separation as a matter of miles. Yet someone can be across the world and remain close to us, while someone in the next room can feel impossibly far away. A road can be a barrier. So can illness, age, fear, politics, or simply the passage of time.

Music is one of the ways I’ve always tried to cross those distances.

I’ve played with people whose languages I didn’t speak, whose musical traditions were different from my own, and whose lives intersected with mine for only a few hours or days. Sometimes the connection was brief, but it was real. We didn’t have to agree about everything, or even explain ourselves very much. We could listen and respond.

That’s part of what I wanted to express in Under the Same Moon.

The moon doesn’t recognize borders. It shines over families who are together and families who are apart, over familiar streets and unfamiliar landscapes, over places we can reach and places we cannot.

I like to imagine someone looking up at it and thinking about another person who might be looking at it too.

It doesn’t erase the distance. It doesn’t solve the political divisions or bring anyone home. But it offers a moment of connection, even if that connection exists only in memory, imagination, or hope.

And sometimes a song is the closest we can come to crossing a border.
