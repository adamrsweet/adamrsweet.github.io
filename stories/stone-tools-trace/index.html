Stone Tools’ Trace

How a few chipped stones are rewriting the story of the First Americans

How did the first people get to North America? For a long time, the textbook answer was a land bridge and an ice-free corridor. The stone tools are telling a different story.

The research

In October 2025, a team including Oregon State University archaeologist Loren Davis published a study in the journal Science Advances. They compared stone tools from North American sites dated between about 20,000 and 13,500 years ago. They found that these tools share a style, and that the earliest projectile points of this kind show up around 20,000 years ago in Hokkaido, in northern Japan.

That supports the idea that the First Peoples of North America came from East Asia along the Pacific coast during the last ice age, likely using boats, before an inland route through the ice sheets had opened.

Davis put it this way: the study "puts the First Americans back into the global story of the Paleolithic – not as outliers – but as participants in a shared technological legacy."

Why I wrote it

I love a story where a small, patient clue changes the big picture. A flake of stone, shaped by someone's hands 20,000 years ago, can still tell us where they walked and sailed. "Stone Tools' Trace" follows that coast, "the sea to their right, ice to their side."

The oldest stories in the Americas are written in stone. Researchers like Loren Davis are learning to read them, and I'm glad to sing along.
