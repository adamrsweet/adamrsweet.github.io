The Temple of Amun-Ra

Karnak began as an island in the Nile, and I once stood there

In 1999 I spent a week in Egypt, playing at a world music festival at the Great Pyramids. I had time to explore, and I made my way to the Temple of Amun-Ra at Karnak. I've never forgotten it.

The research

In October 2025, an international team led by Dr Angus Graham of Uppsala University, with several researchers from the University of Southampton, published the most complete study yet of how Karnak came to be. They took 61 sediment cores from in and around the temple and studied tens of thousands of pottery fragments.

Here's what they found. Before about 2520 BCE, the site flooded too often with fast Nile water for anyone to live there. Then river channels cut their beds to the east and west, leaving an island of high ground. That island became the foundation for the first temple, likely during the Old Kingdom. The researchers suggest the island may echo the "primeval mound" of Egyptian creation stories, the first land to rise from the waters.

Lead author Dr Ben Pennington called it the evolution of Karnak "from a small island to one of the defining institutions of Ancient Egypt."

Why I wrote it

When I read that, I thought of standing among those columns more than twenty-five years ago, with no idea the ground under me had once been an island. "The Temple of Amun-Ra" is a short song about a temple "born from silt and clay."

What they said

— Steve Williams, University of Southampton

The bottom line

Even the greatest temple in Egypt started as a patch of dry ground in a river. Good things often start small.
