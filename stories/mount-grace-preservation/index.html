Mount Grace Preservation

Two hundred acres on Lake Rohunta, forty years of saving land, and a song for the people who did it

Mount Grace Land Conservation Trust turns 40 this year. It's celebrating the best way a land trust can: by inviting all of us outside.

What happened

In December 2025, Mount Grace accepted the deed to the "Eagleville Barrens," nearly 200 acres of woods and wetlands along the shores of Lake Rohunta in Orange and New Salem. The timber company W.D. Cowls, which had long managed it as a working forest, gave it to Mount Grace with money to help care for it. Kestrel Land Trust, Lyme Timber and The Trustees helped make it happen.

It's open to the public for hiking and hunting. The old log roads could become one of the longest accessible trails in the North Quabbin. The sandy, gravelly soil was left by melting glaciers about 10,000 years ago. Mount Grace hopes to bring back the rare pine barrens that once grew there, using careful controlled burns, for birds like the whip-poor-will and the barrens buckmoth.

Executive Director Emma Ellsworth described walking it in winter: "Walking the log roads, seeing bobcat and turkey tracks, and arriving at the snow-covered lake all make for a magical journey full of potential."

Why I wrote the song

"Mount Grace Preservation" is part of my Conservation series. It follows the long arc of that land, from glaciers to working forest to permanent protection, and the quiet teamwork that made it happen. Some places should stay open and whole for everyone who comes after us.

"Not owned by one, not sold away / But kept by patient hands."

How you can help

For the 40th, Mount Grace has an MG40 Trails Challenge: 40 hikes on protected land, at your own pace. Sign up at mountgrace.org.

The bottom line

Forty years of patient work has kept North Quabbin land open for all of us. Happy anniversary, Mount Grace.
